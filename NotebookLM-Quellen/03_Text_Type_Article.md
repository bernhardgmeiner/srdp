# Text type: Article (AHS & BHS)

_This source is part of the "Don't Panic, It's Just the Matura" B2 Writing Guide (matura.bernhardgmeiner.com). It follows the official Austrian SRDP documents for the written English Matura (AHS and BHS, level B2)._

Write for real readers. Long or short task.

## Quick facts

- **Word count:** ~250 or ~400 words
- **Register:** Depends on the audience
- **Title:** Required – catchy!
- **Structure:** ~5 paragraphs

## How to lay it out

- **Catchy title** — rhetorical question, alliteration, bold statement
- **Introduction** — ~50 words – grab the reader's attention immediately
- **Body paragraph 1** — ~50–80 words – bullet point 1
- **Body paragraph 2** — ~50–80 words – bullet point 2
- **Body paragraph 3** — ~50–80 words – bullet point 3
- **Conclusion** — ~50 words – leave the reader thinking

## Do

- Know your audience (teenagers? general public? experts?)
- Use rhetorical questions to engage readers
- Address the reader directly ("you")
- Talk about personal experiences and emotions
- Use a topic sentence for each paragraph
- Write a conclusion that leaves something to think about
- Make the opening paragraph irresistible
- Use exclamation marks and direct speech if the register allows it

## Don't

- Don't be overly emotional or dramatic
- Don't forget who you're writing for
- Don't write a boring title
- Don't write like a formal report (no "It is believed that…")
- Don't ignore the publication context given in the task

## Key tip

Key difference – report vs. article: a report only informs. An article makes the reader feel; it is personal, emotional and engaging. Always check WHERE the article will appear – a school magazine needs a different voice than a serious newspaper.

## Useful phrases

**Openings**

- Have you ever…?
- Do you remember the first time that…?
- Are you one of those people who…?
- There is nothing worse than…

**Engaging throughout**

- Can you imagine…?
- If you ask me,…
- You may not agree with me, but…
- I will never forget the time when…
- To be honest, it scares me when I think that…

**Conclusions**

- So next time you…
- The question is not whether… but when.
- Perhaps it's time we all…
- Food for thought, isn't it?

## Weaker vs. stronger

- Weaker: "In this article I am going to tell you about the advantages of doing sport."
- Stronger: "Ask anyone who has hit the wall at kilometre 30: running is at least half a mental game."

_Why it matters:_ An article has to pull the reader in. Announcing your plan reads flat, while a concrete hook makes someone want to read on. That is exactly what the task rewards.

## Annotated model text

**Model text: "Screens Off, Life On?"**  
_~250 words · Semi-formal (school magazine) register_

> Are you one of those people who check their phones within five minutes of waking up? If so, you are definitely not alone. A recent experiment at our school challenged students to give up their phones for an entire week. The results were surprising.

- "Are you one of those people who check their phones within five minutes of waking up?" — Rhetorical question – grabs reader immediately
- "The results were surprising." — Hook – reader wants to know more

> When the experiment started, most participants felt anxious and disconnected. Several students admitted they instinctively reached into their pockets dozens of times a day. "I didn't even realise how addicted I was," one participant told me. However, by day three the first cracks began to show: a handful of students started talking during breaks instead of reaching for their phones. By the middle of the week, the mood had completely changed. Students who normally sat in silence were suddenly chatting, laughing and swapping stories at every break.

- ""I didn't even realise how addicted I was,"" — Direct speech – suits articles, NOT reports
- "However," — B2 linking word
- "the mood had completely changed" — Turning point in the story

> The most striking change was in the social atmosphere. Without screens to hide behind, students had to look at each other and hold real conversations. I know this sounds old-fashioned, but it genuinely felt different – warmer, somehow. Teachers noticed the difference too, reporting fewer disruptions and more engagement. One classmate of mine, usually glued to her screen, ended up organising a lunchtime card tournament that half the year joined.

- "I know this sounds old-fashioned, but it genuinely felt different – warmer, somehow." — Personal voice – suits articles, NOT reports

> So, would I recommend a phone detox? Absolutely, even if only for a few days. You might be surprised by what you notice when you finally look up from that little screen. Honestly, the hardest part was not giving up the phone. It was admitting just how much I had been missing.

- "So, would I recommend a phone detox?" — Rhetorical question to conclude
- "You might be surprised by what you notice" — Conclusion – leaves reader thinking

## A second model text

**A second model text: "Why learning a language is worth the struggle"**  
_~250 words · semi-formal_

_This second example has no colour labels. Try to spot the catchy opening question, the personal voice, and the way each paragraph develops one clear idea yourself._

> Have you ever given up on a language because the grammar felt impossible? You are not alone. But after spending a summer working in Italy with barely any Italian, I am convinced that pushing through the hard part is one of the most rewarding things a teenager can do.

> The obvious benefit is communication. Once I could order food, ask for directions and joke with my colleagues, a whole country opened up to me. Suddenly I was not just a tourist staring helplessly at a menu. Now I could actually take part. I will never forget the afternoon an elderly shopkeeper, delighted that a foreigner was even trying, spent twenty minutes teaching me the local word for delicious. That single afternoon taught me more than a month of vocabulary lists ever could.

> What surprised me more, though, was what it did to my confidence. Every small conversation I survived made the next one a little easier. I slowly realised that making mistakes was not really embarrassing. It was just part of the process. By the end of the summer, the language had stopped feeling like a school subject and started feeling like a tool I actually owned.

> So if you are ever tempted to quit, my advice is simple: keep going, and look for chances to use the language in real life, even when it feels awkward. The struggle is temporary, but the doors a second language opens stay open for good.

## Check yourself: quiz

**1. What is the most important feature of an article title?**

- It must be exactly 5 words
- It must summarise the whole text
- **It must grab the reader's attention** ✓
- It must include the topic keyword

_Why:_ Article titles should be catchy: rhetorical questions, alliteration, bold statements, direct address. A boring title loses marks on Task Achievement.

**2. Can you address the reader directly in an article?**

- Never – it's too informal
- **Yes – "you" is expected in many articles** ✓
- Only in the conclusion
- Only if the task says so

_Why:_ Articles should engage readers. Using "you" is a key article feature that distinguishes it from a neutral report.

**3. An article task says it's for a "school magazine for teenagers". What register do you use?**

- Very formal – like a business report
- **Semi-formal to informal – engaging and personal** ✓
- Formal – because it's published
- It doesn't matter for articles

_Why:_ Always match register to audience. A school magazine for teenagers calls for a friendly, engaging, semi-formal or informal tone.

**4. Which of these is an appropriate article conclusion?**

- "Taking everything into consideration, it is my belief that…"
- "The data clearly demonstrates that this phenomenon requires urgent action."
- **"So next time you reach for your phone, ask yourself: is it really worth it?"** ✓
- "I would therefore recommend that you consider this issue carefully."

_Why:_ Articles should leave the reader thinking with a question or personal call to action. The other options sound like report or formal-letter conclusions.

**5. What is the key difference between an essay and an article?**

- Articles are always shorter
- **An essay makes the reader think; an article makes the reader feel** ✓
- Essays have titles, articles do not
- There is no real difference

_Why:_ Essays are fact-based, formal and built on generalisations. Articles are personal, emotional and engaging – they connect with the reader.

**6. The task says your article will appear in a serious national newspaper. What changes?**

- Nothing – articles are always informal
- **Use a more formal, measured register but keep it engaging** ✓
- Turn it into a report with headings
- Add emojis to connect with readers

_Why:_ The publication context controls the register. A national newspaper needs semi-formal language, but the article still has to engage: keep the hook and the direct address where they fit.

## Self-check checklist

- [ ] Title is catchy – rhetorical question, alliteration, or bold statement.
- [ ] Wrote with a clear target audience in mind and matched register.
- [ ] Used at least one rhetorical question to engage the reader.
- [ ] Included at least one personal example or anecdote.
- [ ] Conclusion leaves the reader thinking or calls them to action.
