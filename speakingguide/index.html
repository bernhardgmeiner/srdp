How the paired exam works

In the paired form, two candidates take the oral exam together. Each of you gives a talk on your own. After that, the two of you discuss a task together.

  1. before
    Topic area for your talk

    You draw two topic areas and choose one of them. Your partner does the same.

  2. at least 10 min
    Preparation

    You both prepare at the same time, each for your own talk. No aids are allowed: no dictionary and no phone.

  3. about 5 min
    Individual Long Turn, candidate 1

    You speak on your own about a chart, one or two pictures or two statements and work through three bullets. The examiner interrupts as little as possible.

  4. about 5 min
    Individual Long Turn, candidate 2

    Your partner gives their talk on their own topic.

  5. short
    Topic area for the Paired Activity

    Three topic areas are drawn. The two topic areas of your talks are no longer in the pool. Each of you may cross one out. If you both cross out the same one, the examiner decides.

  6. about 10 min
    Paired Activity

    You have a little time to read the task, but no time to prepare. Then you discuss five options with your partner and try to agree on two.

  • Both parts count the same.
  • Each of you gets your own grade, even though you talk together.
  • Your school publishes the list of topic areas for your class by the end of November. Your teachers write the tasks.
Not every class uses the paired form. In the standard form you prepare for at least 15 minutes, and after your talk you speak with the examiner instead of a classmate. Your school announces in the first eight weeks of your final year which form your class will have. Ask your teacher.
On 7 October 2026 the government announced a minimum threshold of 30 per cent for the oral exam as well as the written one. When it starts and how it is calculated has not been published yet. Government statement

Sources: Prüfungsordnung AHS (RIS) · Structure of the oral exam in modern languages (BMB, PDF). Checked on 2026-11-15. Your teachers and the exam regulations are what counts.

How to use your 10 minutes

Ten minutes is not long. Use the time for a plan you can talk from. A written text does not help you: you will not have time to finish it, and reading it out does not sound like natural speech, which is what examiners want to hear.

  1. Minute 0 to 1
    Read the task

    Read the aspect and the three bullets twice. Look at the chart, the picture or the statements and decide what you will say about them in your first minute.

  2. Minute 1 to 7
    Keywords for each bullet

    Write no more than six words for each bullet. Add one example to each bullet: a person, a place, a number, something that happened to you.

  3. Minute 7 to 9
    First sentence and bridges

    Decide how you will start and how you will move from one bullet to the next. Two short phrases are enough.

  4. Minute 9 to 10
    Last sentence

    Decide on your last sentence now. A talk that ends clearly sounds finished, even if you had to leave something out.

  • Give bullet 1 about one minute. Bullets 2 and 3 are where you can show what you can do with the language.
  • Keywords, not sentences. If you write full sentences, you will read them out.
  • If you go blank during your talk, look at your next keyword and start a new sentence.

What examiners listen for

Your examiners use observation sheets with four criteria. Each one is rated in bands from 0 to 10, and band 6 describes the minimum for B2. The grade itself is decided by the exam board. The descriptions below are written in this site's own words. They are not the official wording.

Task achievement
Erfüllung der Aufgabenstellung
You deal with all three bullets in your talk, and with the situation and the options in the Paired Activity. What you say fits the task. You develop your points with reasons and examples instead of only listing them, and you use the time you are given.
Fluency and interaction
Flüssigkeit und Interaktion
You keep talking at a steady pace. Short pauses to think are normal; long silences and many restarts are what examiners notice. In the Paired Activity you react to your partner, ask questions and help the conversation move towards a decision.
Range of spoken language
Spektrum gesprochener Sprache
You have enough words and structures for the topic and can say things in more than one way. If you can't think of a word, you describe what you mean and carry on.
Accuracy of spoken language
Richtigkeit gesprochener Sprache
Your grammar, your choice of words and your pronunciation are correct enough for listeners to follow you without effort. Mistakes happen at B2. What counts is that they do not get in the way.
Rated sample performances (University of Innsbruck, page in German)
Four videos of students in simulated oral exams at B2, with the bands that expert raters gave them (band 7 to band 10) and their reasons. They show what a strong B2 performance sounds like.

A model Long Turn

One way to do it, by a solid B2 candidate. It is a model for how to build a talk. The task is not in the trainer, so there is nothing to learn by heart.

Individual Long Turn · Travel and Tourism
Holidays near and far

“Why fly for hours? Last summer I went hiking in the mountains with my friends, and it was the best holiday I've ever had.”

Mia, 17 (invented)

“I want to see the world while I'm young. You don't learn much about other cultures in your own country.”

Jonas, 18 (invented)

Give a five-minute talk on the topic of travel and tourism in which you

  1. summarise what the two people say in their statements,
  2. outline some of the reasons why travelling abroad is so popular with young people,
  3. give your own opinion on whether people should fly less, supporting it with examples.
OpeningTopic and plan in two sentences. The listener knows what is coming.

My topic is travel and tourism, and I'm going to talk about holidays near and far. First I'll look at the two statements, then I'll talk about why so many young people want to travel abroad, and at the end I'll tell you what I think about flying.

Bullet 1Reports both statements in her own words and sums up the contrast in one sentence. About one minute.

So, the two statements. Mia and Jonas have quite different ideas about holidays. Mia says you don't need to fly for hours to have a good time. She went hiking in the mountains with her friends last summer, and for her it was the best holiday ever. Jonas sees it the other way round. He wants to see the world while he is young, and he thinks you can't learn much about other cultures if you stay in your own country. So one of them is happy close to home, and the other one wants to go as far away as possible.

BridgeOne sentence that leads to the next bullet.

That brings me to my second point: why is travelling abroad so popular with people my age?

Bullet 2Three numbered reasons (first, second, third), each with a concrete example (her cousin in Japan, a friend's trip to Lisbon, a cheap flight to Barcelona), and one extra point at the end.

I think there are three main reasons. The first one is simply curiosity. When you grow up in a small country like Austria, you want to know what life is like somewhere else: the food, the language, the way people live. My cousin, for example, spent three weeks in Japan last summer, and she still talks about how different everything was, from the trains to the way people say hello. The second reason is social media. Every day we see pictures of beaches in Thailand or streets in New York, and of course you start to think, I want to go there too. A friend of mine booked a trip to Lisbon only because she had seen so many videos about it. And the third reason is the price. Flights have become very cheap. Sometimes a flight to Barcelona costs less than a train ticket to the other end of Austria, which is a bit crazy if you think about it. And, well, there is one more thing. After the Matura many people want a break before they start university or a job, so they travel for a few weeks or even months.

BridgeAnnounces the opinion part and repeats the question from the task.

Now let me come to the last point, my own opinion. Should people fly less?

Bullet 3A clear answer, a reason, the other side, her own experience and three concrete proposals. This is the longest part of the talk.

In my view, yes, they should, but I wouldn't ban flying. Flying is one of the worst things one person can do to the climate, and many flights are only for short holidays. On the other hand, I understand Jonas. You really learn something when you travel. Two years ago I spent two weeks with a host family in Ireland, and I spoke more English there than in a whole year at school. So for me the answer is somewhere in the middle. For short trips in Europe we should take the train. Of course, the train is not always easy. It often takes longer, and if you don't book early, it can be more expensive. But if you count the time you spend getting to the airport and waiting there, the difference is often smaller than people think. From Austria you can reach Italy, Germany or Hungary in a few hours, and with a night train you even save a night in a hotel. And if you fly far away, then you should stay longer and not go to another continent for only one week. I also think governments should make train tickets cheaper and flights more expensive. At the moment flying is often cheaper than the train, and most people just go for the cheapest option.

EndingSums up in two sentences and ends on one clear point.

To sum up, Mia and Jonas are both right in a way. Travelling teaches you a lot, but we can't go on flying like this. If I had to pick one point, it would be this: go by train when you can, and only fly when it's really worth it.

What to notice
  • She leaves the statements after about a minute and spends most of her time on bullets 2 and 3.
  • Her reasons come with examples: a cousin, a friend, a price. In the opinion part she adds her own stay in Ireland.
  • Her opinion is clear, and she also shows that she understands the other side.
  • The language is solid B2 and sounds spoken: "a bit crazy", "and, well", some short, simple sentences.

A model Paired Activity

The beginning and the end of a discussion, shortened. Watch what each speaker does with the partner’s last sentence.

Paired Activity · Free Time
More for teenagers in our town

Your town wants to offer teenagers more to do in their free time. The council has money for two projects and has asked young people for their opinion.

Discuss the following options with your partner:

  • opening a youth café,
  • building a skate park,
  • giving teenagers free entry to the swimming pool,
  • setting up a rehearsal room for bands,
  • opening the school gym for sport in the evening.

Try to agree on two.

You have about 10 minutes.

A

OK, shall we go through the options one by one first and then decide?

Organising the talk
B

Yes, good idea. Let's start with the youth café. What do you think about it?

Asking your partner
A

I like it, to be honest. At the moment there is no place where we can just sit and talk without spending much money. In winter that's a real problem.

Opinion with a reason
B

That's true. And a café could be used for other things as well, for example concerts or game nights. So it's more than one project, in a way.

Agreeing and adding
A

I hadn't thought of that. What about the skate park?

Reacting, then moving on
B

Hmm, I'm not so sure about that one. It's great for people who skate, but that's a small group. And nobody can use it when it rains.

Disagreeing politely
A

I get your point, but don't you think a skate park gets people out of the house? Many of us sit in front of a screen all day.

Answering with a question
B

Fair enough, but sport in the school gym would do the same, and for more people. The gym is already there, so it would be cheap as well.

Comparing two options
A

So you're saying the gym is the better choice for sport, is that right?

Checking that you understand
B

Exactly.

Confirming

[ They discuss the swimming pool and the rehearsal room in the same way. ]

A

We've got about two minutes left. Which two do we go for?

Watching the time
B

For me the café is a must. Do you agree?

Making a proposal
A

Yes, definitely. And for the second one I'd go for the gym, because it's cheap and it's for everybody. Can you live with that, even without the rehearsal room?

Proposing and checking
B

I'd still like the rehearsal room, but I can see that fewer people would use it. OK, let's go for the café and the gym.

Giving way, with a reason
A

Great. So we've decided on the youth café, because it gives us a place to meet all year round, and on sport in the school gym, because it's cheap and open to everyone.

Stating the decision
What to notice
  • They agree at the start on how to work through the task.
  • Nearly every turn picks up what the partner has just said.
  • They disagree about the skate park and stay polite.
  • One of them watches the time, and they say their decision out loud, with reasons.

The six practice topic areas

For each topic area: what it covers, questions to think about, and a quick check before the exam.

Key aspects

school types and school systems · tests, grades and pressure · learning outside the classroom · digital tools in lessons · leaving school early · what makes a good teacher · life after school

Questions to think about

  • What should every student know or be able to do at the end of school?
  • How do tests and grades change the way students learn?
  • What can you learn on a school trip that you cannot learn in a classroom?
  • What can make school hard to finish for some young people?
  • How has technology changed lessons since you started school?
  • What would you change about your school if you were the head teacher?

Ask yourself before the exam

  • Can I describe how the Austrian school system works, with the right words for school types and exams?
  • Can I explain why some students do well at school and others drop out?
  • Can I say what I would change about school and give reasons?

Key aspects

how people follow the news · social media · fake news and fact-checking · advertising and influencers · privacy and personal data · phones in everyday life · newspapers, radio and television

Questions to think about

  • Where do you get your news from, and how do you know it is true?
  • Why do false stories spread so quickly online?
  • How does social media change the way friends and families talk to each other?
  • What do influencers and advertisers want from their audience?
  • Which information about yourself would you never share online, and why?
  • Do we still need newspapers and public broadcasters?

Ask yourself before the exam

  • Can I describe my own media habits and compare them with those of older people?
  • Can I explain how misinformation spreads and how to check a story?
  • Can I give my opinion on rules for phones and social media, with reasons?

Key aspects

climate change · waste and recycling · energy and transport · green spaces in towns and cities · protecting animals and plants · what we buy and throw away · what individuals, schools and governments can do

Questions to think about

  • Which environmental problem do you notice most where you live?
  • Why is it so hard for people to change their habits?
  • Where in your town is the heat hardest to bear in summer, and why?
  • Who can do more for the climate: individuals, companies or governments?
  • What happens to the things you throw away?
  • Which habit would you find hardest to change for the climate, and why?

Ask yourself before the exam

  • Can I describe one environmental problem with the right vocabulary and an example?
  • Can I explain causes and effects, for example of waste or of heat in cities?
  • Can I suggest realistic solutions and say who should pay for them?

Key aspects

choosing a career · applications and job interviews · summer jobs and work experience · youth unemployment · working from home · work and free time · skills for the jobs of the future · equal pay

Questions to think about

  • What matters most to you in a job: money, security, free time or meaning?
  • What do employers look for when they hire someone for a first job?
  • What can you learn from a summer job?
  • How will the world of work be different when you are forty?
  • Is university still the best way into a good job?
  • Would you like to work from home? Why or why not?

Ask yourself before the exam

  • Can I talk about my own plans for the future and explain my choice?
  • Can I explain why it is often hard for young people to find their first job, and what that means for them?
  • Can I weigh up the advantages and disadvantages of working while still at school?

Key aspects

artificial intelligence · smartphones and apps · robots and automation · online safety and privacy · technology at school · technology and jobs · who controls new technology

Questions to think about

  • Which piece of technology could you not live without, and why?
  • What do you use AI tools for, and what don't you use them for?
  • Which jobs can machines do better than people, and which can they not do?
  • How do you protect your data and your accounts?
  • Should there be an age limit for social media or AI tools?
  • Does technology make us more independent or more dependent?

Ask yourself before the exam

  • Can I describe how I use technology in an ordinary week?
  • Can I explain how AI and automation might change school and work?
  • Can I argue for or against rules for technology, such as rules for AI at school?

Key aspects

eating habits · fast food and ready meals · school meals · sport and exercise · sleep and stress · advertising for food and drinks · the role of schools and families

Questions to think about

  • What does a typical day of eating look like for young people in Austria?
  • Why do many people eat fast food, although they know it is often unhealthy?
  • How do sleep, stress and exercise affect how well you learn?
  • Who decides what young people eat: parents, schools, companies or young people themselves?
  • Should unhealthy food be more expensive?
  • What could your school do to help students live more healthily?

Ask yourself before the exam

  • Can I describe typical eating and exercise habits of young people, with examples?
  • Can I explain why many people find it hard to eat healthily?
  • Can I give my opinion on who is responsible for young people's health?

Why there is no AI in the trainer

This trainer has no AI in it and gives you no score. That is on purpose.

Your voice stays with you
Your browser makes the recording on your device. The site has no upload function, so the person who runs the site cannot hear it. If you save a recording as a file, it goes wherever your downloads go. On a shared computer, delete your recordings when you are done.
No scores from a machine
A score from software looks exact, but it does not tell you what your examiners will hear. You listen to yourself and answer questions that examiners also ask themselves. That is slower, and you learn more from it.
The exam has no AI either
In the exam there are you, your partner, the examiners, a task and a clock. The trainer adds only a recorder, so that you can listen to yourself.
