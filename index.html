The Big Picture

How to use this guide: Start with Overview and Grading to understand how the exam works. Then read the section for whichever text type you feel weakest on. Use the Model Texts to see theory in action – hover over the coloured highlights to learn what each part does. Test yourself in the Practice section. And keep the Grammar Kit open while proofreading your practice texts.

In your English Matura, the Writing section gives you 120 minutes for two writing tasks. One will be longer (~400 words), typically an essay or a report. The other will be shorter (~250 words) and could be any of the other text types. Together, writing counts for 25% of your total grade.

Time management: You have 120 minutes for 2 texts. A good split: ~60–65 minutes for the 400-word task and ~45–50 minutes for the 250-word task. Reserve 5–10 minutes at the end for proofreading both texts. Within each task: ~5 min planning, ~35–45 min writing, ~5–10 min checking.

Golden Rule: The word count tolerance is ±10%. For a 400-word task, that's 360–440. For 250 words, it's 225–275. Going over or under by more than 10% costs you one level on Task Achievement.
What counts toward the word count? Every word in the body of your text counts, including the title. Header elements like To/From/Date/Subject in reports and e-mails are not typically counted. When in doubt, count everything – it's safer to be slightly over than under.

Every task will give you three bullet points (content points). You must address all three, roughly equally. Each bullet point should get its own paragraph in the main body.

How You Are Graded

Your writing is assessed on four equally weighted criteria, each scored 0–10. Since 2024, the updated assessment scale (Bewertungsraster 2023) is in use:

CriterionWhat It MeansWhat Examiners Look For
1. Task Achievement
(Erfüllung der Aufgabenstellung)		 Did you do what the task asked? All content points covered and developed; text type conventions and layout followed; appropriate title/greeting/closing; word count observed (±10%); supporting details and examples for each point
2. Coherence & Cohesion
(Kohärenz und Kohäsion)		 Does your text hold together logically? Clear overall structure; effective paragraphing; logical development and connection of ideas; variety of linking devices; clear relationships between sentences and paragraphs
3. Lexical & Structural Range
(Spektrum sprachlicher Mittel)		 How wide is your language? Variety of vocabulary and structures; complex sentence forms; varied formulation (not repeating the same phrases); appropriate register (formal/informal); not lifting words from the prompt
4. Lexical & Structural Accuracy
(Sprachrichtigkeit)		 How correct is your language? Grammar control; correct word choice; spelling; punctuation; accurate use of linking devices; reader doesn't have to stop and re-read
VETO Rule: If you completely miss the task (wrong text type, none of the content points addressed), you score 0 on everything. The other three criteria won't even be assessed. Always read the task carefully.
Missing a bullet point is expensive: If you only address 2 out of 3 content points, your Task Achievement score drops to level 6 at best (out of 10). Address all three – even briefly – before going into detail on any one.
Compensation works in your favour: The four criteria are independent. If your grammar is weaker but your content and structure are strong, the good scores compensate. Play to your strengths.
Don't play it safe with grammar. A common mistake: students use only simple, short sentences to avoid errors. The assessment scale punishes this. If you only write B1-level structures (simple sentences, basic vocabulary), your Range AND Accuracy scores are capped at level 4 – even if everything is perfectly correct. B2 means taking risks: use complex sentences, relative clauses, conditionals, passive voice. A few errors in complex structures score higher than perfect simple ones.

The Opinion Essay

An essay presents your personal opinion on a topic and defends it with arguments and examples. Your goal: convince the reader. You can mention the opposing view, but only to show why it's unconvincing.

Word Count
~400 words
Register
Formal
Title
Required
Structure
5 paragraphs

Layout

Title
Introduction (~80 words) – hook + state your opinion
Body Paragraph 1 (~80 words) – Bullet point 1
Body Paragraph 2 (~80 words) – Bullet point 2
Body Paragraph 3 (~80 words) – Bullet point 3
Conclusion (~80 words) – summarise + restate opinion in different words

The P.E.E.(P.) Formula for Each Body Paragraph

Every body paragraph should follow this pattern:

StepWhat to DoExample
PointTopic sentence – state your argumentMany women find it very difficult to return to work after having children.
Evidence / ExplainSupport it with reasoningThe main reason for this is that there are rarely any provisions made for childcare.
ExampleGive a concrete exampleA recent study shows that fewer than 5% of large companies offer on-site daycare for their employees.
Point (optional)Circle back to your argumentThis leads to the conclusion that workplace childcare must become a priority.

How to Write the Introduction

Start with one of these hooks, then clearly state your position:

A rhetorical question A striking fact or statistic A relevant quotation A controversial statement

✓ DOs

  • Write in a formal style throughout
  • Make your opinion clear from the introduction
  • Start each paragraph with a topic sentence
  • Use generalisations (not personal anecdotes)
  • Use formal linking words: However, Nevertheless, Moreover, Furthermore
  • Vary sentence length – mix short and long
  • Use complex grammar: passive voice, conditionals, relative clauses
  • You may mention a counter-argument – but then refute it

✗ DON'Ts

  • No contractions (don't, can't, it's)
  • No colloquial or slang expressions
  • No overly emotional language ("I absolutely detest…")
  • No personal examples ("When I was 12…") – use general or hypothetical examples instead
  • No over-generalisation ("All politicians are liars.")
  • No simple linking words as your only connectors (and, but, because)
  • No very short sentences one after another
  • No statistics without context

Useful Phrases

Giving your opinion (without overusing "I"):

It is (firmly) believed that… One can argue that… Many people are convinced that… As far as many are concerned,… It is often argued that…

When you do use "I":

In my opinion/view,… I am inclined to believe that… I (firmly) believe that… I am (not) convinced that…

Adding arguments:

Furthermore,… Moreover,… In addition to this,… What is more,… Not only… but also…

Introducing the other side:

It would also be interesting to see… One should, nevertheless, consider… The other side of the coin is that… On the other hand,…

Balanced conclusions:

Taking these points into consideration,… All of this points to the conclusion that… On balance, we can safely say that… The arguments presented prove that…

Key difference – Essay vs. Article: An essay should make the reader think. It is fact-based and formal. An article should make the reader feel. It is more emotional and can be informal.

The Article

An article is a text for publication – a newspaper, magazine, or online journal. It should inform, entertain, persuade, or engage the reader. Think of yourself as a journalist or a contributor to a magazine.

Word Count
~250 words
Register
Formal or Informal (depends on audience)
Title
Required – make it catchy!
Structure
5 paragraphs

Layout

Catchy Title
Introduction (~50 words) – grab the reader's attention
Body Paragraph 1 (~50 words) – Bullet point 1
Body Paragraph 2 (~50 words) – Bullet point 2
Body Paragraph 3 (~50 words) – Bullet point 3
Conclusion (~50 words) – leave the reader thinking

Title Ideas

Your title must grab attention. Try:

A rhetorical question – "Is Technology Killing Conversation?"
Direct address – "What You Always Wanted to Know About…"
Alliteration – "Luscious Lollipops for Cool Kids"
A bold statement – "Technology: A Curse in Disguise"

✓ DOs

  • Know your audience (teenagers? general public? experts?)
  • Use rhetorical questions to engage readers
  • Address the reader directly ("you")
  • Talk about personal experiences and emotions
  • Use a topic sentence for each paragraph
  • Write a conclusion that leaves something to think about
  • Make the opening paragraph irresistible
  • Can use exclamation marks and direct speech if appropriate

✗ DON'Ts

  • Don't be overly emotional or dramatic
  • Don't forget who you're writing for
  • Don't write a boring title
  • Don't write like an essay (no "It is believed that…")
  • Don't ignore the publication context given in the task

Useful Phrases

Openings:

Have you ever…? Do you remember the first time that…? Are you one of those people who…? There is nothing worse than…

Throughout:

Can you imagine…? If you ask me,… You may not agree with me, but… I will never forget the time when… To be honest, it scares me when I think that…

The Report

A report is a formal, factual document written for someone in authority (a boss, a headmaster, a committee). You present findings – often from a survey or investigation – and end with recommendations. This is the most structured text type.

Word Count
~250 or ~400
Register
Formal, objective, neutral
Title
General heading + section headings
Structure
Header + sections with headings

Layout

Date: [date]
From: [your name/role]
Subject: [clear, descriptive title]

Introduction (with heading, ~50/80 words) – state purpose, data source
Section 1 (with heading, ~50/80 words) – Bullet point 1
Section 2 (with heading, ~50/80 words) – Bullet point 2
Section 3 (with heading, ~50/80 words) – Bullet point 3
Conclusion / Recommendations (with heading, ~50/80 words) – summarise + suggest
The actual exam template for reports only has: Date, From, Subject. There is no "To:" field on the Formatvorlage. Don't add one – just fill in what's given. The section headings, however, are mandatory and must be created by you.

✓ DOs

  • Present and analyse facts, not opinions
  • Be neutral, concise, and to the point
  • Use the passive voice where appropriate
  • Include specific data when referencing surveys/statistics
  • End with clear recommendations or suggestions
  • Make it the most organised-looking text on your paper
  • Use linking words to show proficiency

✗ DON'Ts

  • Don't give personal opinions in the main body (save "In my opinion" for the conclusion only)
  • Don't repeat information
  • Don't write anything unnecessary
  • Don't forget the Date/From/Subject header
  • Don't forget section headings
  • Don't use emotional or informal language

Useful Phrases

Introduction:

As requested, I have prepared a report on… The aim of this report is to… This report examines/compares… The data was collected through…

Presenting data:

Twenty per cent of the people asked… The majority of respondents… One out of five stated that… It is generally felt that… A large proportion of those interviewed…

Conclusion / Recommendations:

On the basis of these findings,… I would therefore recommend/suggest that… It would seem that… is the best option. In view of this, one can recommend…

  • Address the reader directly
  • Encourage readers to comment

Engaging the reader:

E-Mail Sub-Types

📨 Letter / E-Mail of Complaint

You're unhappy with a product or service and you want something done about it.

Key Phrases

Opening:

I am writing to express my dissatisfaction with… I am writing to complain about… I would like to draw your attention to…

Describing the problem:

…was not what we had been led to expect It was not up to standard We were appalled to find… To make matters worse,…

Making demands:

I would like you to refund my money. I expect a letter of explanation. Unless I receive a satisfactory reply, I will… I would be grateful if you could…

Closing:

I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience. Thank you for your cooperation in correcting this matter.

📨 Letter / E-Mail of Application

You're applying for a job, internship, or course. Be confident, professional, and specific.

Key Phrases

Opening:

I am writing to apply for the position of… as advertised in… I am writing with reference to your advertisement… I am interested in applying for the position of…

Qualifications & Experience:

I am currently a [age]-year-old student at… My qualifications include… For the past [time], I have… I gained experience while… As you can see from the attached CV,…

Skills & Why You're Suitable:

I believe I am suitable for the position as I… I consider myself to be enthusiastic, hard-working and reliable. I have excellent communication skills.

Closing:

I am available for an interview at your convenience. I look forward to hearing from you. Please find my CV attached.

Tenses tip: Use Present Simple for skills (I am a reliable person), Past Simple for past experience (I worked for…), Present Perfect for recent activities (I have been working…).
📨 Letter to the Editor

You respond to a published article or comment. This is one of the most frequently tested e-mail sub-types. You must reference the original publication, then agree or disagree with arguments.

Layout Specifics

To: [e.g. editor@guardian.com]
From: [your email]
Date: [date]
Subject: [topic of the article you respond to]

Dear Sir or Madam, / Dear Editor,

Introduction – name the publication, the article, its date. State your position.
Body (3 paragraphs) – support your view with arguments, examples, personal experience
Conclusion – restate opinion, make an appeal or call to action

Yours faithfully,
[Name]
[Town/City]
Two greeting options: Both Dear Sir or Madam, and Dear Editor, are correct for a letter to the editor. Both close with Yours faithfully, since you don't use a personal name.

Key Phrases

Opening – always reference the article:

I am writing in response to the article on…, which appeared in… on [date]. I am writing with reference to the article you published in last month's issue. I have just read your article on… and I feel I must respond. You raised some issues which I feel strongly about.

Agreeing:

I completely agree with you that… I wish to congratulate you on this article. It is true that… I am sure readers will agree with me when I say that…

Disagreeing:

I am afraid I totally disagree. It is simply not true that… I cannot agree with you about… The author fails to mention… While I completely agree that…, I cannot accept…

Conclusion / Call to action:

I hope that… Considering the arguments presented, we should… What we must do is make sure that… May I draw your attention to…

Make it personal and local: Explain why you care about this issue. Explain how it affects your community. This makes your letter stand out and shows genuine engagement.
📨 E-Mail of Enquiry / Requesting Information

You want information about something – a product, a service, an event, etc.

Key Phrases

I am writing to enquire about… I would like to request further information about… Could you possibly tell me…? I would appreciate it if you could send me… I look forward to receiving the information requested.

General E-Mail Rules (All Sub-Types)

✓ DOs

  • Include all header elements (To, From, Date, Subject)
  • State your reason for writing in the first sentence
  • Use formal language – no slang
  • Use full forms (do not instead of don't)
  • Address the reader as "you"
  • Leave a blank line between paragraphs
  • Match greeting to closing (Dear Sir → Yours faithfully)
  • Use "softer" verbs when criticising: rather than, I would like to point out, it seems to me

✗ DON'Ts

  • No contractions in formal e-mails
  • No emojis or emoticons
  • No casual greetings (Hey, Hi there)
  • Don't use a silly e-mail address (katzi1@gmx.at)
  • Don't forget a subject line
  • Don't mix up faithfully/sincerely

General Tips for All Text Types

Before You Write

1. Read the task twice. Underline the text type, the audience, the three bullet points, and the word count. Getting any of these wrong is catastrophic.

2. Plan before you write. Spend 5 minutes making a quick plan. Jot down which arguments/ideas go where. This prevents rambling and forgotten bullet points.

3. Decide on register immediately. Formal = no contractions, no slang, passive voice welcome. Informal = contractions OK, personal tone, direct address.

While You Write

4. One bullet point = one paragraph. This is the simplest way to guarantee you cover all content points roughly equally.

5. Topic sentences. Start every body paragraph with a clear sentence that tells the reader what this paragraph is about.

6. Linking devices – use variety. Don't just use and, but, because. Show B2 level:

FunctionBasic (avoid)B2 Level (use these)
Addingand, alsoFurthermore, Moreover, In addition, What is more, Not only… but also
ContrastingbutHowever, Nevertheless, On the other hand, Nonetheless, Although, Despite
CausebecauseSince, As, Due to the fact that, Owing to
ResultsoTherefore, Consequently, As a result, Thus
ExamplelikeFor instance, For example, Take for example, A case in point is
Conclusionto finishTo sum up, Taking everything into account, All things considered

7. Vary your grammar. Show range by using:

Passive voice Conditional sentences (If…) Relative clauses (who, which, that) Participle constructions Reported speech Inversions (Not only… but also…)

8. Don't lift from the prompt. The task gives you words and phrases. Rephrase them in your own words. The assessment scale explicitly penalises frequent lifting: if you copy-paste phrases from the task, your Range score drops to level 4 or below. Even if your grammar is perfect, recycling the prompt's language signals limited vocabulary.

After You Write

9. Count your words. Seriously. Count them. ±10% is the safe zone.

10. Proofread for your personal mistakes. You know your weaknesses – articles? third person -s? tenses? Check specifically for those.

11. Check text type conventions. Quick mental checklist: Does my report have section headings? Does my email have a subject line and proper greeting? Does my essay have a title?

Understanding the Task: Operators

The task instructions use specific verbs – operators – that tell you exactly what to do. Misreading these costs you Task Achievement points. Here's what each one demands:

OperatorWhat You Must DoExample Formulation
discussLook at a topic from different angles. Present arguments for and against, weigh them up.There are strong arguments both for and against… On the one hand… On the other hand…
evaluateExamine something and judge its quality or value. Say whether it's good or bad, effective or not – and why.While this approach has certain merits, it fails to address… Overall, it can be considered…
argue (for/against)Take a clear position and support it with reasons. Stronger than "discuss" – you're not neutral.I firmly believe that… The most convincing reason for this is…
analyseBreak something down into its parts or causes. Look at how and why something works (or doesn't).The main factors contributing to this are… A closer look reveals that…
comparePoint out similarities and differences between two or more things.While X tends to…, Y is more likely to… Both share the characteristic of…
comment onGive your opinion or reaction to something. Less structured than "discuss" – more personal.What strikes me most about this is… In my view, this is…
describeSay what something is like. Give details, features, characteristics. Factual, not opinionated.The programme consists of… It features… Participants are expected to…
suggest / recommendPropose ideas or solutions. What should be done? What would improve the situation?I would recommend that… One possible solution would be to… It might be worth considering…
explainMake something clear and understandable. Say how or why something happens.The reason for this is that… This is because… This can be explained by the fact that…
point outDraw attention to specific facts, problems, or aspects. Highlight what matters.It is important to note that… One aspect that deserves attention is… What is often overlooked is…
Multiple operators in one task? A single bullet point might say "evaluate the advantages and suggest improvements". That means you need to do both – judge the current state AND propose changes. Underline every operator in the task before you start writing.

Essay ~400 words · Formal · Title required

The Hidden Cost of Fast Fashion TITLE

Have you ever stopped to think about where your clothes actually come from? RHETORICAL QUESTION HOOK The fashion industry has grown dramatically over the past two decades, with consumers now purchasing COMPLEX STRUCTURE around 60% more clothing than they did in 2000. In my opinion, the environmental and social damage caused by fast fashion far outweighs the benefit of cheap clothing. THESIS – OPINION STATED CLEARLY

To begin with, the environmental impact of fast fashion is staggering. TOPIC SENTENCE = POINT The production of cheap garments requires FORMAL VERB enormous amounts of water and energy, and releases toxic chemicals into rivers and soil. According to LINKING the United Nations Environment Programme, the fashion industry is responsible for approximately 10% of global carbon emissions – more than international flights and maritime shipping combined. EVIDENCE + EXAMPLE Furthermore, B2 LINKING most of these garments end up in landfill within a year of purchase, creating mountains of textile waste that will take centuries to decompose.

Equally alarming are the working conditions in the factories that produce these clothes. TOPIC SENTENCE Workers in countries such as Bangladesh and Cambodia are often forced to PASSIVE work twelve-hour shifts for wages that barely cover basic living costs. The Rana Plaza factory collapse in 2013, which killed over 1,100 garment workers, RELATIVE CLAUSE exposed the dangerous reality behind bargain-priced T-shirts. CONCRETE EXAMPLE Despite B2 LINKING promises of reform, investigations continue to reveal forced overtime and unsafe buildings.

Admittedly, CONCESSION MARKER some argue that fast fashion makes trendy clothing accessible to people who cannot afford designer brands. COUNTER-ARGUMENT ACKNOWLEDGED While LINKING this may be true to some extent, it does not justify an industry built on exploitation and pollution. Affordable clothing can exist without disposable clothing – brands such as Patagonia and Uniqlo have shown that durable, fairly produced garments do not have to be expensive. COUNTER-ARGUMENT REFUTED

Taking these points into consideration, CONCLUSION LINKER it is clear that the true cost of fast fashion is paid not by consumers, but by workers and the planet. If we genuinely want to CONDITIONAL address the climate crisis, we must start by rethinking our relationship with the clothes we wear. CONCLUSION – OPINION RESTATED IN NEW WORDS

Screens Off, Life On? CATCHY TITLE (question)

Are you one of those people who check their phone within five minutes of waking up? RHETORICAL QUESTION – GRABS READER If so, you are definitely not alone. When the experiment started, most participants felt anxious and disconnected. TOPIC SENTENCE Several students admitted they instinctively reached into their pockets dozens of times a day. "I didn't even realise how addicted I was," DIRECT SPEECH – suits articles one participant told me. However, LINKING by day three, something shifted: people started talking more during breaks, playing cards, and actually paying attention in class.

The most striking change was in the social atmosphere. TOPIC SENTENCE Without screens to hide behind, students had to look at each other and hold real conversations. I know this sounds old-fashioned, but it genuinely felt different – warmer, somehow. PERSONAL EXPERIENCE – suits articles, NOT essays Teachers noticed the difference too, reporting fewer disruptions and more engagement.

So, would I recommend a phone detox? DIRECT ADDRESS + RHETORICAL Q Absolutely – even if CONCESSION only for a few days. You might be surprised by what you notice when you finally look up from that little screen. CONCLUSION – LEAVES READER THINKING

Text A – "Essay" by a student

Should Schools Ban Mobile Phones?

Hey guys! So there's been a lot of talk about banning phones in schools lately. I think it's a terrible idea and here's why.

6 errors found:
1. "Hey guys!" – colloquial greeting. Essays are formal. No direct address.
2. Contractions (there's, here's, it's, couldn't) – not allowed in formal essays.
3. Personal anecdote ("When my school tried…", "My best friend Lea") – essays use general examples, not autobiographical stories.
4. "loads of apps" – colloquial. Formal alternative: "a large number of applications".
5. "phone bans are dumb" – emotive/slang. Use: "banning phones is unlikely to be effective".
6. "!!!" – exclamation marks are inappropriate in formal essays.
Overall: This reads like a blog post, not an essay. Wrong register throughout.
Text B – "Report" by a student

From: Student Council

2. "Dear Sir" – reports don't have a greeting. That's for emails/letters.

6. "fire the catering company" – too aggressive. Reports make measured recommendations: "It would be advisable to review the current catering contract."

Grammar Kit for Austrian Learners

These are the most common mistakes that German-speaking students make in English. They come from L1 interference – your brain trying to apply German rules to English. Knowing them means you can catch them when you proofread.

1. Articles: a / an / the / (nothing)

German has articles too, but English uses them differently. This is the single biggest error source for Austrian students.

The school is very important for young people. (German: "Die Schule ist wichtig" → tempting to add "the")
School is very important for young people. (general concept = no article)
I go to the school every day. (German: "Ich gehe in die Schule")
I go to school every day. (institution = no article)
The life is complicated.
Life is complicated. (abstract concept = no article)
She plays the piano very well. ✓ BUT: She plays the football.
She plays the piano. ✓ She plays Ø football. (instruments: THE / sports: no article)

Quick rule: Use "the" when both you and the reader know which specific one. Use "a/an" when introducing something new. Use no article for abstract concepts, general plurals, sports, meals, and institutions used for their purpose (school, church, hospital, prison).

2. Prepositions

German and English prepositions rarely match 1:1. Don't translate – learn the English collocations.

I am good in English.
I am good at English.
I am interested for this topic.
I am interested in this topic.
It depends from the weather.
It depends on the weather.
I am waiting on the bus.
I am waiting for the bus.
She consists on going there.
She insists on going there. (consists of = besteht aus)
We discussed about the problem.
We discussed the problem. (discuss = transitive, no preposition)
In the one hand… in the other hand…
On the one hand… on the other hand…

4. Since vs. For

German uses "seit" for both. English distinguishes between a point in time and a period of time.

I have been living here since five years.
I have been living here for five years. (period of time → for)
I have been living here for 2019.
I have been living here since 2019. (point in time → since)

Since = starting point (since Monday, since 2019, since I was a child). For = duration (for two hours, for five years, for a long time). Both trigger Present Perfect (or Present Perfect Continuous).

5. If vs. When

German "wenn" covers both. English splits them.

If I come home, I always eat dinner. (routine → not uncertain)
When I come home, I always eat dinner. (it definitely happens → when)
When it rains tomorrow, we will stay inside. (uncertain → not definite)
If it rains tomorrow, we will stay inside. (might or might not happen → if)

When = it will definitely happen (just a question of timing). If = it might or might not happen (condition).

6. Make vs. Do

German uses "machen" for almost everything. English splits it.

I have to make my homework.
I have to do my homework.
She does a lot of mistakes.
She makes a lot of mistakes.

DO = tasks, work, activities (do homework, do the dishes, do exercise, do a favour, do your best, do business). MAKE = creating/producing something (make a mistake, make a decision, make money, make a suggestion, make progress, make an effort, make friends).

7. Say vs. Tell

He said me that he was tired.
He told me that he was tired.
He told that he was tired.
He said that he was tired.

Say = say something (no person directly after). Tell = tell someone something (always needs a person after it). He said (that)… / He told me (that)…

8. Present Perfect vs. Past Simple

German Perfekt ("Ich habe gemacht") looks like Present Perfect but often translates as Past Simple in English.

I have seen this film yesterday.
I saw this film yesterday. (specific past time → Past Simple)
I went to London three times (and might go again).
I have been to London three times. (life experience, no specific time → Pres. Perfect)
She has graduated in 2020.
She graduated in 2020. (finished past event with specific time → Past Simple)

Past Simple = finished action at a specific time (yesterday, in 2020, last week, when I was 10). Present Perfect = connected to the present (ever, never, already, yet, just, since, for, so far, this week).

9. The Forgotten Third-Person -s

German doesn't add -s in the third person. English does. Every single time.

She think that this is a good idea.
She thinks that this is a good idea.
The report show that 60% of students agree.
The report shows that 60% of students agree.

This is the easiest point to lose and the easiest to fix during proofreading. Scan every sentence: if the subject is he/she/it or a singular noun, the verb needs -s.

10. Word Order

German has flexible word order (verb-second rule, time before place). English is much stricter: Subject – Verb – Object – Manner – Place – Time (or Time at the very beginning of the sentence).

I wrote yesterday a test. (German: "Ich schrieb gestern einen Test")
I wrote a test yesterday. / Yesterday, I wrote a test. (time goes at the end or very beginning)
There are in Austria many beautiful mountains.
There are many beautiful mountains in Austria.

Also watch out for adverb placement: adverbs of frequency go before the main verb but after "be": She always forgets / She is always late. And remember: cannot is one word in English, not two (can not is only correct when "not" belongs to what follows: "You can not only read but also write").

11. Adjective vs. Adverb

In German, adjectives and adverbs often look the same ("schnell"). In English, adverbs usually take -ly.

She drives very careful.
She drives very carefully. (how does she drive? → adverb)
He speaks English perfect.
He speaks English perfectly.
She can sing really good.
She can sing really well. (good = adjective, well = adverb of "good")

Exceptions that never change: fast, hard, well, late, early. And remember: good is always an adjective (a good singer), well is its adverb (she sings well).

12. Much / Many / A Lot Of

There are much students in the class.
There are many students in the class. (countable plural → many)
I don't have many time.
I don't have much time. (uncountable → much)

Many = countable plural (many books, many people). Much = uncountable (much water, much time, much money). In positive statements, prefer a lot of for both: "There are a lot of students" sounds more natural than "There are many students."

13. Conditionals – Would + Infinitive, Not Would + Past

If I would have more time, I would study abroad.
If I had more time, I would study abroad. (Type 2: if + Past Simple, would + infinitive)
If I would be you, I would accept the offer.
If I were you, I would accept the offer.

Never put "would" in the if-clause. The structure is: If + Past Simple, … would + infinitive (Type 2) or If + Past Perfect, … would have + past participle (Type 3).

Second pass: prepositions and articles. Third pass: spelling of tricky words. You will catch far more errors this way than by reading "generally".
